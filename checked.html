What we checked

Every paper, dataset and piece of code we have checked, with the outcome, whether or not we found an error. What we choose to check, and why, is explained on the How it works page.

When we find a possible error, the authors, data producers or developers hear about it first. Until they reply or three weeks pass, such items are counted here but not named; then they are added by name with a link to their register entry. Papers · Datasets · Code

Mainstream findings

  • Henley et al. 2024, Nature: Coral Sea ocean heat highest in four centuries (Great Barrier Reef). No error found. Reproduced from the authors' code and figure data, including the bound on the pre-1900 maximum.
  • Cai et al. 2024, Nature Geoscience: Summer warming on the Mongolian Plateau, 1986–2004, boosted by internal variability. No error found. Warming rate (about 3.6 times the Northern Hemisphere land average) reproduced in two CRUTEM5 versions; unchanged with unadjusted station data and a stable station network.
  • Wang et al. 2025, Science: Canadian forests more conducive to high-severity fires in recent decades. No material error found. Trend tests and false-discovery-rate control checked; the ERA5 fire-weather inputs were not recomputed.
  • Haslinger et al. 2025, Nature: Increasing hourly heavy rainfall in Austria and flood changes. No error found. Tested for rain-gauge sensor changes with the authors' station data; the increase stands.
  • Bevacqua et al. 2025, Nature Climate Change: A year above 1.5 °C and the Paris Agreement limit. No error found. Idealized experiment reproduced, including with year-to-year persistence.
  • Byrne et al. 2024, Nature: Carbon emissions from the 2023 Canadian wildfires. No error found. Warmest/driest and fossil-emission comparisons checked against independent datasets.
  • Bevacqua et al. 2024, Nature Geoscience: Direct and lagged effects of the 2022 European drought. No error found. Design reviewed; no testable error.
  • Vicente-Serrano et al. 2025, Nature: Mediterranean precipitation: high variability, not trend. No error found. Regional trends, significance tests and the effect of homogenization reproduced from the authors' data.
  • Wang et al. 2024, Science: Anthropogenic influence on river-flow seasonality. No material error found. Alaska stations agree with the paper.
  • Gottlieb & Mankin 2024, Nature: Human influence on Northern Hemisphere snow loss. No material error found
  • Kannenberg et al. 2024, Nature Geoscience: Soil moisture and dryland carbon and water fluxes. No material error found. Method reviewed; flux data not rerun (login required).
  • Esper, Torbenson & Büntgen 2024, Nature: 2023 summer warmth unparalleled over 2,000 years. Already known, corrected or challenged in print. A slip in Table 1 had already been corrected by the authors (May 2025).
  • Zhou, Leung & Lu 2024, Nature Geoscience: Steady threefold Arctic amplification. Already known, corrected or challenged in print. Already challenged in a published comment, with the authors' reply.
  • England et al. 2025, Nature: Drivers of the extreme North Atlantic marine heatwave of 2023 (weak winds, shoaling mixed layers). No material error found. The main result is a model heat budget (ACCESS-OM2), not rerun here. Minor: the observed summer mixed-layer shoaling trend (−0.58 m per decade, IAPv3) is fitted by ordinary least squares and called significant without allowing for autocorrelation; the paper itself notes that products disagree on the long-term trend.
  • Benz et al. 2024, Nature Geoscience: Global groundwater warming due to climate change (conduction model, 1880–2100). No material error found. The global model runs in Google Earth Engine and was not rerun. The validation (R² 0.75, RMSE 1.4 °C against about 10,000 wells and 72 boreholes) tests absolute groundwater temperatures, not warming, and most of its skill comes from differences between climates: within single countries the model often has little or no skill (for example R² below zero in Austria, Belgium and the Netherlands; Austria and Switzerland about 1.8 °C too cold). This matters most for the population counts above drinking-water temperature thresholds, which depend on local absolute values.
  • Greene et al. 2024, Nature: Ubiquitous acceleration in Greenland Ice Sheet calving, 1985–2022 (1,034 Gt lost to retreat). No material error found. Not reproduced (the ice masks need an Earthdata login; the full text is paywalled). One point of definition: the 1,034 Gt is the total mass of ice in the retreated area, most of it below flotation, while consensus mass-balance estimates (gravimetry, altimetry) measure mass above flotation by design, so the 'underestimated by as much as 20%' comparison sets two different quantities side by side. The authors acknowledge this in the abstract (minimal direct impact on sea level), and their code computes the above-flotation share.
  • Luo et al. 2024, Nature: Drought triggers and sustains overnight fires in North America (GOES, 2017–2020). No material error found. Main analysis uses one satellite (GOES-16) over 2017–2020, so there is no sensor change inside it. We tested whether ranking the 2017–2020 events against a 2000–2020 baseline that contains those same drought seasons could create the reported shift toward conducive fire weather; in a no-trend simulation the effect is under one percentage point and of the wrong sign. Minor: the paired Wilcoxon test treats the 1,095 events as independent, although most come on consecutive nights within the same fires.
  • Nearing et al. 2024, Nature: Global prediction of extreme floods in ungauged watersheds (Google flood forecasting vs GloFAS). Already known, corrected or challenged in print. Already challenged in print: Li, Razavi, Maier, Hrachowitz et al. 2026, Journal of Hydrology X 30, 100215 (doi:10.1016/j.hydroa.2026.100215) — event thresholds taken from modelled rather than observed flows, a ±2-day hit window, no testing beyond 10-year events, high false-alarm and miss rates, and a single global benchmark. We did not repeat that work.
  • Jarvis & Forster 2024, Nature Geoscience: Human-induced warming estimated from the linear relationship between global temperature and atmospheric CO2 (1.49 ± 0.11 °C in 2023, pre-1700 baseline). No material error found. Reproduced from HadCRUT5, Law Dome and Mauna Loa (slope 1.06 °C per 100 ppm, intercept −0.54 °C, 2023 estimate 1.50 °C). A perfect-model test with a two-layer energy-balance model driven by AR6 forcings, across climate sensitivities of 2.1–4.9 °C and aerosol forcing of −0.4 to −1.7 W/m², recovered the true human-induced warming to within about ±0.07 °C except at the weakest aerosol forcing (+0.08 to +0.15 °C too high), and the stated uncertainty mostly covered the truth. Minor: the stated uncertainty does not include the choice of temperature dataset (Berkeley Earth gives 1.57 °C, HadCRUT5 1.50 °C), and the code link given in the paper no longer resolves.
  • Terhaar et al. 2025, Nature: Record jump in global sea surface temperature in 2023–2024: return period from AR(1) synthetic series and from 270 climate-model runs. No error found. Read the full Methods. The obvious selection risks are addressed: the end-of-series trend estimate is checked against model runs that contain such jumps (bias 1 ± 18%), the choice of starting month is tested (margins change by under 0.01 °C), and the return period is recomputed with a second SST dataset (ESA CCI/OSTIA: 543 years vs 512) and with AR(2) and MA(1) models (484 and 498 years). The model-based estimate uses an exact binomial interval. The jump threshold equals the observed margin, which is the usual way to frame such a question. Not rerun.
  • Van Passel et al. 2024, PNAS: Critical slowing down of the Amazon forest (EVI lag-1 autocorrelation trends) and drought frequency, intensity and duration. No material error found. Method reviewed; data not rerun. Multi-month drought pulses can raise lag-1 autocorrelation in a moving window without any change in recovery rate. In an idealised simulation with a fixed recovery rate this reproduced part of the reported pattern (the intensity effect) but not the duration or frequency-by-intensity effects, and the authors' second method, which accounts for rainfall forcing, supports their conclusion. Not material on current evidence.
  • Cunningham et al. 2025, Science: Trends in socially and economically disastrous wildfires, 1980–2023 (top 200 by loss as share of GDP, plus events with ≥10 deaths). No material error found. Method reviewed (preprint, EcoEvoRxiv v2); data not public (NatCatSERVICE, EM-DAT login), so not rerun. Losses are normalised by national GDP and the count trend is fitted with a count regression on year. The main caveats are stated by the authors: possible reporting bias in the early record, growing exposure (wildland–urban interface) not modelled as a covariate, and a climate link that rests on fire-weather conditions at the time of the events rather than a formal attribution.

Papers questioning mainstream findings

  • McKitrick & Christy 2020, Earth and Space Science: Pervasive warming bias in CMIP6 tropospheric layers (38 models vs satellites, radiosondes and reanalyses, 1979–2014). No material error found. Trend comparisons use a heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-robust (Vogelsang–Franses type) test, so the significance claims allow for serial correlation in both model and observed series. The finding that CMIP6 historical runs warm the troposphere faster than observed over 1979–2014 agrees with mainstream assessments. The observational comparison series share inputs, so 'every target observational analog' is not a set of independent tests, and the step from trend differences to lower climate sensitivity also depends on how far the observed sea-surface warming pattern differs from the models'. Not rerun.

The share with errors therefore says nothing about how often datasets in general contain errors.
