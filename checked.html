What we checked

Every paper, dataset and piece of code we have checked, with the outcome, whether or not we found an error. What we choose to check, and why, is explained on the How it works page.

When we find a possible error, the authors, data producers or developers hear about it first. Until they reply or three weeks pass, such items are counted here but not named; then they are added by name with a link to their register entry. Papers · Datasets · Code

Papers

Papers taken up in our systematic screening since 22 November 2026. Papers questioning mainstream climate findings are tested with the same methods and standards as the rest. The numbers are small, so the proportions say little yet. "Method reviewed, not rerun" means we read the methods and found nothing that called for a rerun; it is a lighter check than "no error found", which means we reproduced or tested the result.

  • Luo et al. 2024, Nature: Drought triggers and sustains overnight fires in North America (GOES, 2017–2020). No material error found. Main analysis uses one satellite (GOES-16) over 2017–2020, so there is no sensor change inside it. We tested whether ranking the 2017–2020 events against a 2000–2020 baseline that contains those same drought seasons could create the reported shift toward conducive fire weather; in a no-trend simulation the effect is under one percentage point.
  • Nearing et al. 2024, Nature: Global prediction of extreme floods in ungauged watersheds (Google flood forecasting vs GloFAS). Already known, corrected or challenged in print. Already challenged in print: Li, Razavi, Maier, Hrachowitz et al. 2026, Journal of Hydrology X 30, 100215 (doi:10.1016/j.hydroa.2026.100215) — event thresholds taken from modeled rather than observed flows, a ±2-day hit window, no testing beyond 10-year events, high false-alarm and miss rates, and a single global benchmark. We did not repeat that work.
  • Jarvis & Forster 2024, Nature Geoscience: Human-induced warming estimated from the linear relationship between global temperature and atmospheric CO2 (1.49 ± 0.11 °C in 2023, pre-1700 baseline). No material error found. Reproduced from HadCRUT5, Law Dome and Mauna Loa (slope 1.06 °C per 100 ppm, 2023 estimate 1.50 °C). In a perfect-model test with a two-layer energy-balance model across AR6 ranges of climate sensitivity and aerosol forcing, the method recovered the true human-induced warming closely.

  • Schöngart et al. 2025, Nature Climate Change: Contributions of high-income groups to warming and to heat and drought extremes. No material error found. Checked with the authors' Zenodo data (14860538): extra 1-in-100 heat events attributed to the top 10% and top 1% are 69% and 22% of the total, close to their 65% and 20% shares of attributed warming; the metric counts additional events, so it does not have the near-zero-baseline problem of probability ratios.
  • Waidelich et al. 2024, Nature Climate Change: Climate damage projections beyond annual temperature (10% of GDP at +3 °C). No material error found. Reran the main regression (the Kotz et al. 2022 specification) from the authors' Zenodo files. The regional GDP panel contains implausible growth values (Uzbekistan 1996–99 at +37% to +61% per year in log terms; tenfold jumps in some Kenyan, Spanish and Malaysian regions), but dropping Uzbekistan or all |Δlog GDP| > 0.5 changes the temperature effect by under 0.1 percentage points. No material error found.
  • Childs et al. 2025, PNAS: Climate warming is expanding dengue burden in the Americas and Asia (18% of cases attributed to past warming). No material error found. Refitted the main Poisson fixed-effects model on the shareable part of the data (Zenodo; mostly Brazil) and added future temperatures as a placebo: the lead terms are near zero (−0.016 against +0.76 for the lagged response at 20–21 °C). One robustness model (4th-order polynomial) repeats the lag-1 term instead of lags 2 and 3; this affects only that check. No material error found.
  • Sui et al. 2024, PNAS: Global scale assessment of urban precipitation anomalies (over 60% of cities wetter than their surroundings). No material error found. The paper has no placebo test, so we ran one with IMERG V07 (2001–2020 mean) and 1,487 GHS-SMOD urban centers: 59.7% of cities are wetter than their outer rural ring (paper: 63%), against 46.1% for the same discs moved away from any city. The urban excess holds for inland (56% vs 45%) and coastal (68% vs 51%) cities separately. No material error found.
  • Qin et al. 2025, Nature: Impact of Amazonian deforestation on precipitation reverses between seasons (WRF 2000 vs 2020 forest cover, 2001–2022). No material error found. The paper's error bars are the standard error across spatially correlated grid cells, and there is no ensemble to measure chaotic model noise. Using the authors' figshare output we reproduced every buffer mean exactly (e.g. wet season +5.76 mm/month at 0 km, −0.28 at 60 km; dry season −1.55 at 40 km), then recomputed the uncertainty from the 22 independent yearly run pairs, which include the model's chaotic noise. The year-based errors are similar to or smaller than the published ones, and the sign of each effect is stable across years (e.g. the 60 km wet-season decrease: p = 0.008, 17 of 22 years negative). No material error found.
  • Josey et al. 2024, Nature: Record-low Antarctic sea ice in 2023 increased ocean heat loss and storms (ERA5, four ice-loss boxes, June–July). No material error found. Using the authors' Zenodo archive (10.5281/zenodo.13971927) we reproduced the June–July storminess index for the four boxes (days with 10-m wind above 10 m/s): 11.6 days in 2023 against 9.0 ± 0.9 in 1990–2015, the highest of 1990–2023 (with area weighting, 2015 is slightly higher). In 1990–2022, the boxes' storm days are not correlated with their sea-ice concentration or net heat loss (r ≈ −0.1, p ≈ 0.5), so the storm link rests on 2023 alone; the paper says that its storm result is consistent with, but does not establish, a causal link. The heat-loss and dense-water results are not affected. No material error found.
  • Edwards et al. 2025, Science: Rapid butterfly declines across the United States during the 21st century (22% fewer butterflies, 2000–2020). No material error found. The Supplementary Materials say that standard errors for each species' overall rate of change were propagated across years assuming zero covariance between years. Because every year's rate is built from the same regional coefficients, that assumption would make the standard errors far too small if applied literally. We checked the published values in Tables S5 and S9: for the 192 species fitted in more than one region, the overall standard error is about as large as, or larger than, the largest regional standard error (median ratio 0.96), so the reported uncertainties are not deflated and the species classifications are not affected. No material error found.
  • Callahan et al. 2025, Nature Climate Change: Increasing risk of mass human heat mortality if historical weather patterns recur (August 2003 at 1.5 °C: about 17,800 deaths in one week). No material error found. Using the authors' Zenodo archive (10.5281/zenodo.17495872) we reproduced the peak-week Europe-wide deaths for the August 2003 pattern (8,940, 17,570 and 31,480 at 0, 1.5 and 3 °C; paper 9,000, 17,800 and 32,000). The exposure–response function is a fourth-order polynomial fitted to 2015–2019, and most of the projected deaths come from district-weeks hotter than anything that district saw in those years (81% of the peak week at 1.5 °C, 92% at 3 °C), so the results rest on extrapolation. As an out-of-sample check, in the 416 districts with 2003 mortality data the observed excess deaths in weeks 31–34 of 2003 were about 15,800, against model values of 7,500 (pre-industrial) and 15,600 (1.5 °C) for that pattern, so the extrapolated response does not overstate deaths for a real event outside the fitting period. No material error found.
  • Turubanova et al. 2025, PNAS: Unprecedentedly high global forest disturbance due to fire in 2023 and 2024. No material error found. The data producers have warned that Global Forest Change loss detection became more sensitive after a 2011 method change and Landsat 8 (2013), and the paper's 2002–2024 trends span those changes. We summed the authors' fire-loss layer and all forest loss (tree cover ≥30% in 2000) by year and latitude zone in Google Earth Engine. Global fire loss does step up at 2011–2012 (3-year means rise by about 65%, mostly in boreal forest, where all loss rises too), but the increase continues at the same rate within the consistent period: about +5.5% per year over 2012–2024 against +5.4% over 2001–2024, and 2020–2024 averages 12.9 Mha a year against 8.3 Mha in 2012–2016. There is no step at 2022 (Landsat 9). The headline increase and the 2023–2024 records do not depend on the detection changes. No material error found.
  • Krumpen et al. 2025, Nature Climate Change: Smoother sea ice with fewer pressure ridges in a more dynamic Arctic (airborne laser altimetry, 1993–2023). Method reviewed, not rerun; no material error found. We looked at whether the step in ridge density around 2007–2010 could come from changes of sensor and platform (aircraft-mounted altimeter in the 1990s, towed EM-Bird later, fixed-wing towing from 2009). The paper's own control argues against an instrument artefact: ridge density, height and spacing of first- and second-year ice stay stable across the whole period, which a change in detection would also have affected. Trends use mixed models with campaign effects and autocorrelation.
  • Pan et al. 2024, Nature: The enduring world forest carbon sink (3.6 / 3.6 / 3.5 Pg C per year by decade). No material error found. Recomputed the 1990s-to-2010s biome changes and their uncertainties from Table 1 and the USDA data archive (RDS-2023-0051), treating decades as independent as the paper's own trend test does. The global sink result is unaffected. No material error found.
  • Wu et al. 2025, Nature Climate Change: Decreasing dynamic predictability of global agricultural drought with warming climate. No material error found. Read the Supplementary Information and the archived figure code (Zenodo 12737359) and simulated the significance test applied to sliding-window skill (Mann–Kendall with trend-free pre-whitening, ensemble-agreement rule). The model inputs are not archived, so the headline could not be rerun. No material error found.
  • Chai et al. 2025, Nature Climate Change: Constrained Earth system models show a stronger reduction in future Northern Hemisphere snowmelt water. No material error found. Checked the hierarchical emergent-constraint code against Bowman et al. (2018) and recomputed from the archived fields (GitHub): models' historical SWE is 1.55× the observations, and the raw future ensemble is 1.34× the constrained value, consistent with the paper. No material error found.
  • Wang et al. 2025, Nature Climate Change: Dual impact of global urban overheating on mortality (cold benefit more than fourfold the heat cost). No material error found. Recomputed from the archived city table (GitHub, 3,276 cities): the urban heat island's reduction in cold-related mortality is 4.6× its increase in heat-related mortality (paper: more than fourfold). The ratio follows from the standard temperature–mortality curves, in which moderate cold carries most attributable deaths. No material error found.
  • Price et al. 2025, Nature: Probabilistic weather forecasting with machine learning (GenCast). Method reviewed, not rerun; no material error found. Read the methods (arXiv v2): significance is tested with a stationary block bootstrap with automatic block length, so serial correlation between forecast dates is handled; each system is verified against its own analysis, and the look-ahead difference is stated. Forecast archives (WeatherBench 2) were not reachable from our workspace, so nothing was rerun.
  • Allen et al. 2025, Nature: End-to-end data-driven weather prediction (Aardvark Weather). Method reviewed, not rerun; no material error found. Read the paper and the released code (GitHub aardvark-weather-public): training (to 2017), test (2018) and validation (2019) years are kept separate in the data loaders; station forecasts are tested on held-out years at stations seen in training, as the paper states. Gridded forecasts, including HRES and GFS, are verified against ERA5. Nothing was rerun.
  • Zeng et al. 2019, Nature Climate Change: A reversal in global terrestrial stilling and its implications for wind energy production. Already known, corrected or challenged in print. Part of the post-2013 wind-speed increase in the station data was later traced to an undocumented change in how calm periods were encoded, which over-estimated average wind speeds in much of Asia and Europe (Dunn, Azorin-Molina, Menne, Zeng et al. 2022, Environ. Res. Commun. 4, 061003, doi:10.1088/2515-7620/ac770a). Instrument changes in the US network are the subject of our separate ASOS note (Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.24357793).
  • Liu et al. 2026, Nat Commun: Intense tropical-cyclone season length. Error found (see register)

Where the model reports its column-water spurious source (5 of the 17 cases) it is at round-off level, and clipping of negative water is negligible in all cases. Results change at long time steps (20 minutes), but that is already documented in print (Wan et al. 2021, GMD; Santos et al. 2021, JAMES), and the climate models run CLUBB in 5-minute substeps.
  • MOM6 equations of state and freezing point (main, 20 Nov 2026): Ocean model (GFDL, NCAR CESM3) seawater density options. No error found. All eight density options compared with TEOS-10 (GSW) or EOS-80 over temperature, salinity and pressure up to 6000 dbar: differences are the published fitting errors of each formula, the TEOS-10 option matches exactly, and the density derivatives agree with finite differences everywhere. Freezing points agree with TEOS-10. The developers' own documented known bugs (old WRIGHT option, a GSW second-derivative issue) were not counted.
  • CVMix KPP (fce4221, May 2026): Ocean boundary-layer mixing (MOM6, CESM, E3SM MPAS-Ocean). Method reviewed, not rerun; no material error found. Read the core K-profile code against Large, McWilliams & Doney (1994): stability functions, their coefficients and continuity, velocity scales including the pure-convection limit, the shape-function coefficients and the unresolved-shear term all match. Code reviewed and key formulas recomputed, not run in a model.
  • YSU boundary-layer scheme, NCAR MMM-physics (d524bc9, 18 Nov 2026): Planetary boundary layer mixing (WRF, MPAS). No error found. Ran the scheme on 180 test columns (time steps 30–900 s, surface heat flux −50 to 400 W/m², evaporation, winds 0.5–15 m/s): potential temperature and water vapour are conserved to round-off, matching the surface fluxes. The heat tendency is returned as temperature and converted back correctly in the MPAS wrapper.
  • Thompson microphysics sedimentation, CCPP physics (b332cc8): Falling rain and graupel in GFS/UFS (semi-Lagrangian scheme). No error found. Extracted the sedimentation routine and ran 40,000 random columns (layers 20–1,200 m, fall speeds up to 20 m/s, time steps up to 300 s): mass in the column plus surface precipitation is conserved to about one part in 10⁸ (the tiny minimum-value floor), and no negative values. Unlike the WSM6 routine, whose mass loss others have reported. The rest of the scheme was not checked.
  • Noah-MP land model: snow and soil freezing and melting, soil water budget, canopy radiation (NCAR/noahmp 5a32aaf, 18 Nov 2026; CCPP copy b332cc8): Land surface model (UFS/GFS, WRF, MPAS, HRLDAS). No material error found. Ran the freezing and melting routine on 400,000 random snow and soil states, and the full model for a year at Bondville, Illinois, plus a colder, snowier version of the same year (4,500 steps with layered snow and a spring melt). Energy in the phase-change step is conserved to round-off, and the water balance error is at most 0.002 mm per step. The supercooled soil-water solver agrees with an exact solution to 0.0006 m³/m³. The canopy radiation scheme gave no negative absorption over 4,608 sun, leaf-area and albedo combinations per option. The 2-m temperature issue Noah-MP shares with the GFS surface layer has been sent to the developers.
  • MYNN-EDMF boundary-layer scheme (CCPP physics b332cc8; NCAR MMM-physics d524bc9 for WRF and MPAS; upstream NCAR/MYNN-EDMF 9a35d5d): Planetary boundary layer and shallow-cumulus mixing (HRRR, RRFS, WRF, MPAS). Already known, corrected or challenged in print. Ran 6,000 column steps (surface heat flux −40 to 400 W/m², winds 0.5–15 m/s, with and without the mass-flux plumes) and checked the heat, moisture and momentum budgets. Two errors in the CCPP and WRF/MPAS copies: (1) when negative cloud water from the plume transport is removed, its latent heat is counted twice, creating heat (mean 25 W/m², up to about 900 W/m² in single steps of our cloudy test columns); (2) the surface air density uses T + 0.608q instead of T(1 + 0.608q), so surface heat, moisture and momentum fluxes enter about 1% too strongly in humid air. Both were already fixed in the upstream code by its developers (October and November 2026, versions 6.1.0–6.1.1); the copies in CCPP and MMM-physics have not yet been updated (a WRF update is pending). With the first error corrected and the scheme's deliberate dissipative heating left out, the liquid-water potential temperature budget closes to 0.02 W/m².
  • GFDL fast saturation adjustment, fv_sat_adj (CCPP physics b332cc8): Condensation, freezing and melting inside the FV3 dynamical core (GFS/UFS with GFDL microphysics). No error found. Ran the adjustment on 20,000 random grid boxes (190–315 K, 150–1,050 hPa, 50–130% relative humidity, all six water species, some small negative values from transport), hydrostatic and non-hydrostatic, intermediate and last steps, with and without the condensate-loading build option. Total water and moist internal energy (with temperature-dependent latent heats) are conserved to round-off in every case; temperature changes up to 8.5 K. Negative cloud ice that cannot be filled from snow is passed on to graupel, which is the routine's documented filling order, not a loss.
  • GFDL cloud microphysics v3 and its coupling to FV3 in the UFS (CCPP physics b332cc8; FV3 dev/emc 0492f16): Cloud microphysics in the UFS suites FV3_GFS_v16_gfdlmpv3 and FV3_HAFS_v1_gfdlmpv3_tedmf. Already known, corrected or challenged in print. Ran 2,000 random cloudy columns. Inside the scheme, water is conserved to 10⁻¹⁴ kg/m² and its own dry-energy check passes at 10⁻¹³. But in the physics-suite path the scheme returns humidities relative to the new (dry + vapour) air mass, and the UFS driver multiplies them by the old layer mass, so water species gain or lose mass in proportion to the vapour that condensed or evaporated (up to 0.02–0.03 kg/m² per step in our deliberately supersaturated test columns). The older 2019 version scales its tendencies correctly. This was already found by others: a fix (rescaling by the new/old layer mass) and a second fix for rain falling with the ice heat capacity are in an open pull request to ufs-community/ccpp-physics (#413, October 2026).
  • Zhang–McFarlane deep convection, CAM CCPP version (ESCOMP/atmospheric_physics 0e23c43, 20 Nov 2026): zm_convr, zm_conv_evap, zm_conv_convtran: Deep convection in CESM (CAM) and E3SM. No error found. Ran 600 random soundings (tropical ocean, tropical land and cold continental; 264 convecting, rain up to 68 mm/day, surface snow up to 16 mm/day). Water and energy balance to round-off in each step, using CAM's own convention: the convective core balances surface precipitation plus detrained liquid, and the rain-evaporation step balances evaporation and the heat of fusion carried by surface snow. Convective tracer transport conserves tracer mass to 10⁻¹⁷ of the column, for tracers defined on moist or dry air. The momentum transport routine was not tested.
  • Zhang–McFarlane deep convection, E3SM EAM version (E3SM master 9b3897d, 24 Nov 2026): zm_conv_main and zm_conv_evap, with and without convective microphysics: Deep convection in E3SM v3 (EAM; also the basis of the EAMxx C++ port). No error found. Ran 1,800 random soundings (tropical ocean, tropical land, cold continental) in three set-ups: the E3SM v3 default (dCAPE trigger, unrestricted launch level, dynamic closure adjustment and convective microphysics on, with bulk sulfate for droplet and ice nucleation), the same without microphysics, and the base EAM defaults. Rain up to 80 mm/day, snow up to 12 mm/day, detrained ice up to 7.6 mm/day. Water and energy balance to round-off in every case, using E3SM's own energy-checker accounting (precipitation plus reserved liquid, snow plus reserved ice). Tracer and momentum transport were not tested.

  • NOAA GHCN-Monthly v4 production source, including the Pairwise Homogenization Algorithm (NCEI release of 17 Mar 2025): Station temperature homogenization behind NOAAGlobalTemp and GISTEMP. Method reviewed, not rerun; no material error found. Read the break detection (SNHT statistic and its critical-value tables), model selection (BIC/AIC, t and F tests with their tables), the Tukey-hinge median of pairwise adjustment estimates, the sort routine, how adjustments are applied segment by segment, and the z-score and spatial-corroboration quality control. All match Menne & Williams (2009) and the published QC. Two known design choices: SNHT critical values are held at the n = 250 value for longer series, and the tests do not allow for autocorrelation; the algorithm relies on confirmation across many station pairs instead. Code reviewed, not built or run; neighbour selection and changepoint merging were not read in detail.
  • ILAMB 2.7 (rubisco-sfa/ILAMB, commit 8e58622) and ilamb3 (commit 8f720a2): Land-model benchmarking scores. No material error found. Read the bias, RMSE, seasonal-cycle, spatial-distribution and interannual-variability scores and the weighting of the overall score; they match Collier et al. (2018). One latent inconsistency: for site (point) data, ILAMB 2.7 builds its interannual-variability score from anomalies about the time mean only, so the seasonal cycle is counted as interannual variability, whereas the gridded path removes the annual cycle first. The score is off by default (skip_iav = True) and no shipped configuration turns it on for site data; ilamb3 has no interannual-variability score. Code reviewed, not run.
  • xMIP (cmip6_preprocessing), commit cb8262b: CMIP6 preprocessing and control-run drift removal. No material error found. Read the drift removal (linear trend in the parent control run from the branch point, aligned by the CMIP6 branch-time metadata) and the longitude, grid and vertex fixes. A synthetic control run with a known drift, branched into historical and scenario runs, was detrended exactly. With NumPy 2.5 the drift-removal step stops with a TypeError (a date-index type, fixable in one line), which gives no wrong results. Longitude 0 is relabeled 360, which the package handles.
  • CDO 2.4.0 (Climate Data Operators, MPI-M): ETCCDI percentile indices (TX90p etc.). No error found. Ran ydrunpctl/eca_tx90p and the etccdi_tx90p operator on a synthetic 60-year AR(1) series with a 1961–1990 base. The eca_* operators have no bootstrap, so the in-base exceedance rate is biased low (9.9% vs 10.6% out of base), the known Zhang et al. (2005) effect; this is documented, and CDO's etccdi_* operators apply the bootstrap, giving 10.6% in base vs 10.6% out of base. ydrunpctl percentiles agree with exact percentiles to within about 0.15 K (0.7 K at the ends of the year). No error found.
  • MetPy 1.7.1 (Unidata), CAPE/CIN family: CIN, LFC/EL choice, mixed-layer and most-unstable parcels, downdraft CAPE. No material error found. Read cape_cin, lfc, el, mixed_parcel and downdraft_cape and ran 500 random soundings with capping inversions and mid-level stable layers. CAPE and CIN follow the documented definitions (net area LFC to EL; net area below the LFC, set to zero if positive). One issue: lfc() and el() compute the LCL from the parcel's virtual temperature when called from cape_cin, placing it 13–44 hPa too high. This is already reported, with a fix, in MetPy PR #4067 (May 2026). With that fix, CAPE changed in 52 of 473 soundings by at most 79 J/kg (median 4 J/kg), CIN by at most 12 J/kg. Earlier checks (12 Nov) found surface-based CAPE and the LCL correct.
  • Py-ART 2.3.0 (ARM-DOE/pyart), calculate_attenuation_zphi: ZPHI rain attenuation correction. Already known, corrected or challenged in print. Tested with a self-consistent synthetic test (true specific attenuation, path attenuation, ΦDP and measured reflectivity all generated together). The routine puts a ΦDP-pre-corrected reflectivity into the ZPHI integral where the method requires the measured one, so attenuation is put in the wrong places along the ray. Corrected reflectivity in echoes above 35 dBZ is too low by 0.4 dB on average at C band (5.5 dB path attenuation) and 1.2 dB on average, 3.2 dB at most, at X band (10 dB path attenuation); the one-line fix brings both within about 0.1 dB. The error had already been reported in Py-ART issue #883 (2019); we added the quantification, a verified fix and a second, smaller indexing error to that issue on 25 Nov 2026.
  • Py-ART 2.3.0 (ARM-DOE/pyart), beam geometry and grid_from_radars: Radar gate locations and gridding. No error found. Gate heights and ground ranges from antenna_to_cartesian match the Doviak–Zrnić 4/3-earth formulas exactly (0.5–20° elevation, 10–250 km). Gridding a known linear field from a 10-elevation volume with the Barnes, Barnes2, Cressman and nearest-neighbour weightings gives mean errors under 0.05 units; the larger local errors come from the gaps between elevation angles, a limitation of the method, not a coding error.
  • Py-ART 2.3.0 and wradlib 2.9.8, KDP retrievals: Specific differential phase from ΦDP. No material error found. Synthetic ΦDP from known KDP (three rain cells per ray, noise 0–3°). The wradlib regression and Lanczos derivatives, wradlib's phidp_kdp_vulpiani, and Py-ART's kdp_vulpiani and kdp_maesaka recover the KDP integral to within 0.3% without noise. Under noise the methods with physical thresholds overestimate it (+5% to +16%), as their constraints imply. Py-ART's kdp_schneebeli overestimates narrow cells by about 11% even without noise, which appears to be a property of its constrained filter. One small bug: wradlib's 'finite_difference_vulpiani' derivative divides by the window length instead of the gate separation (factor (n−1)/n); it is only used internally as a first guess.
  • TempestExtremes (ClimateGlobalChange/tempestextremes, commit cafb070): Cyclone detection and tracking (DetectNodes, StitchNodes). No material error found. Synthetic sea-level-pressure lows at known positions: all positions were found within 0.4° (including across the dateline and the prime meridian and up to 88°N and 84°S), all tracks were complete, and the minimum-duration and maximum-gap settings behave exactly at their limits. Two traps for users, not errors in results we could find: StitchNodes assumes the standard calendar unless --caltype is given, so noleap model output with gaps given in hours splits storms that cross 28 February in years divisible by 4 (the workflows we checked give gaps as step counts, which are unaffected); and with packed (quantized) pressure, equal-valued neighboring minima are kept as duplicate detections unless --mergeequal is set, though the stitched tracks were unaffected.
  • atlite (PyPSA/atlite, commit 936ef28), bundled wind-turbine power curves: Wind power curves used by PyPSA-Eur. No error found. We tested all 27 bundled power curves against the Betz limit (no turbine can extract more than 16/27 of the wind's kinetic energy). Every curve is physically possible: for each turbine, the smallest rotor that could deliver the tabulated power is smaller than the real rotor (for example the Vestas V164 needs at least 134 m against its real 164 m). The highest implied power coefficient was 0.55 (Siemens SWT-3.6-107), below the limit. This contrasts with the Open Energy Platform curves in windpowerlib, three of which exceed the limit (see the register).
  • pvlib-python 0.16.1, bundled CEC module and inverter databases: Solar PV module and inverter parameters. No material error found. We checked the bundled CEC module and inverter parameter tables for physically impossible or inconsistent values. Inverters: clean. Modules: a few bookkeeping oddities that do not affect modeled output, such as half-cut-cell modules listing 120 or 144 cells in series (the CEC model does not use this field) and some tiny negative short-circuit temperature coefficients. No error that would change a simulation materially.
  • pysteps 1.21.5 (radar nowcasting): Verification scores, rain-rate conversions, motion estimation, extrapolation, cascade and noise. No error found. Fractions skill score, all categorical scores (POD, FAR, CSI, ETS, HSS, SEDI and others), ensemble CRPS, rank histogram (including many dry pixels), ROC area and reliability diagrams all match independent reference calculations. Z–R and decibel conversions invert exactly; the Lucas–Kanade motion field recovers a known displacement to within 0.01 pixel per step; semi-Lagrangian extrapolation reproduces a translated field (error under 1% of its spread); the cascade decomposition reconstructs the field exactly; and the noise generator reproduces the input power-spectrum slope (−2.99 against −3.0).
